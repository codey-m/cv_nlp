A working reference for course developers

Visual methods for course design

What should a learner be able to see? Choose a comparison, a relationship or a piece of evidence first, then the visual method that makes it inspectable.

14 methods. The sample visuals are authored schematics. Their marks and lengths are illustrations, with no study observations encoded.

Use this catalog
  1. Start with the learner's question and action. A unit opener raises a question; a summary connects methods; a lab view helps inspect evidence.
  2. Compare each method's encoding, inputs and tradeoffs. Use the source examples to study a mechanic, then build a treatment suited to the unit.
  3. Keep source evidence, authored layout and measured geometry distinct. Mark missing observations explicitly and provide a readable still or text route.

14 methods

01 · Explanatory diagram

Same-scene layers

Switch explanatory views around the same example.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Switch explanatory views around the same example.
What the marks mean
Object identity and reviewed categorical highlights. Position and area imply no measured amount.
Inputs needed
The same source condition in both views, with checked descriptions for each layer.
Tradeoffs
A toggle asks readers to remember the previous view. Offer a simultaneous still comparison.

Source mechanics: Wonky · A visual history of every Air Jordan · The Naked Truth

02 · Many-to-many diagram

Linked category lanes

Select an item and follow its connections across stable categories.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Select an item and follow its connections across stable categories.
What the marks mean
Equal-width connectors show reviewed associations, including many-to-many links.
Inputs needed
An explicit relation table that permits several links and unresolved classifications.
Tradeoffs
Crossing lines grow quickly. Reveal a selected neighborhood; widths imply quantities only when justified.

Source mechanics: How do animals sound across languages? · Wonky

03 · Comparison layout

Matched small multiples

Inspect repeated views beside one another.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Inspect repeated views beside one another.
What the marks mean
Consistent framing and marks; quantitative panels share a valid scale.
Inputs needed
Matched source conditions and a reason to compare the panels.
Tradeoffs
Too many panels dilute the comparison. Keep a recurring example and a small first view.

Source mechanics: Women's Pockets are Inferior · How do animals sound across languages?

04 · Qualitative reveal

Expanding context

Open a short label into the circumstances needed to understand it, then fold it back.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Open a short label into the circumstances needed to understand it, then fold it back.
What the marks mean
Nested context and a persistent label. Panel size and phrase length are editorial layout.
Inputs needed
Verified examples, exact attribution and checked explanations.
Tradeoffs
A reveal can become a wall of text. Let a few precise clauses and source examples carry it.

Source mechanics: The Structure of Stand-Up Comedy · A visual history of every Air Jordan

05 · Object atlas

Example contact sheet

Select one example in a contact sheet to inspect its condition, source and evidence.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Select one example in a contact sheet to inspect its condition, source and evidence.
What the marks mean
One tile per exact example; ordinary tile order and area imply no measured distance or intensity.
Inputs needed
Source-backed examples, distinct conditions and explicit omissions.
Tradeoffs
Pictures can blur meaningful distinctions. Keep the original label and condition visible.

06 · Qualitative evidence lens

Usage in context

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action

What the marks mean
Aligned wording and separately attributed contexts.
Inputs needed
Verified excerpts and source context, with qualified translation review where needed.
Tradeoffs

07 · Bipartite graph

What the marks mean
Two node types and exact supplied links; equal-width links with counts on selection when available.
Inputs needed

09 · Pair evidence layout

Shared-example bridge

Bring two items together through the exact examples they share.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Bring two items together through the exact examples they share.
What the marks mean
Identity and membership in supplied lists; aligned positions are authored.
Inputs needed
Exact shared labels, original supporting values and known omission states.
Tradeoffs
An item missing from a supplied list is not necessarily absent. Avoid implying complete similarity.

10 · Paired bars / dots

Paired count strips

Compare paired bars or dots, then inspect the source record.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Compare paired bars or dots, then inspect the source record.
What the marks mean
Position or length on one count scale, with original values and unknown markers for missing entries.
Inputs needed
Exact counts, units and source-collection limits.
Tradeoffs
Counts may lack a common denominator. Never invent percentages or turn omitted entries into zeros.

11 · Pairwise overview

Progressive relationship matrix

Learn one pairwise relationship, reveal its row, then open the full grid.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Learn one pairwise relationship, reveal its row, then open the full grid.
What the marks mean
One named relationship and scale; each cell links to its supporting evidence.
Inputs needed
The complete intended matrix, explicit ordering and a consistent cell meaning.
Tradeoffs
A grid alone is an inspector. Explain one cell first; mirrored cells are not independent observations.

12 · Unit chart

Counted unit tiles

Gather or compare repeated marks, with the exact total available.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action

What the marks mean
A declared number of recorded units per mark.
Inputs needed
Exact counts, an explicit counting unit and consistent scaling.
Tradeoffs
Marks cannot silently change from mentions to people or independent samples. Large counts need a visible conversion.

13 · UpSet

Intersection plot

Select a few sets, inspect an intersection and reveal its members.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Select a few sets, inspect an intersection and reveal its members.
What the marks mean
Membership dots plus counts of exact members, with individual set sizes retained.
Inputs needed
Exact set membership and explicit coverage or omission limits.
Tradeoffs

14 · Projection / spatial navigation

Semantic neighborhood map

Explore nearby items while inspecting their original similarities and support.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Explore nearby items while inspecting their original similarities and support.
What the marks mean
Coordinates from named data and a projection; spatial distances are approximate.
Inputs needed

Tradeoffs

Give the visual a job in the unit.

The same visual method can serve different activities. Choose its role in the learning sequence before designing its controls.

Unit opener

Expose the question before its vocabulary. Give learners a simple decision whose consequence makes a new idea useful.

Mid-lecture explainer

Make a mechanism just taught visible. Compare two states, then check understanding on a case the readout does not show.

Unit summary

Hold one example across the unit's methods, with each step addressing a visible limitation. Place it before the problem set and keep it unassessed.

Lab evidence view

Let learners inspect a controlled comparison and the evidence behind a claim. Preserve units, uncertainty, missingness and access to original values.
