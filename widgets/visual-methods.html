Visual methods for course design

What should a learner be able to see? Choose a comparison, a relationship or a piece of evidence first, then the visual method that makes it inspectable.

17 methods. The sample visuals are authored schematics. Their marks and lengths are illustrations, with no study observations encoded, and each card's worked example uses made-up data.

Use this catalog
  1. Start with the learner's question and action. A unit opener raises a question; a summary connects methods; a lab view helps inspect evidence.
  2. Compare each method's encoding, inputs and tradeoffs. Use the source examples to study a mechanic, then build a treatment suited to the unit.
  3. Keep source evidence, authored layout and measured geometry distinct. Mark missing observations explicitly and provide a readable still or text route.
  4. Pairings on each card are examples, not a menu. Any action can meet any visual method; the test is whether the learner can see why their action mattered. Each card names actions from the interaction methods catalog that it can carry, and its recipes pair a visual method with an action for one learning question.

The methods

17 methods

01 · Explanatory diagram

Same-scene layers

Switch explanatory views around the same example.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Switch explanatory views around the same example.
What the marks mean
Object identity and reviewed categorical highlights. Keep the object and its position fixed, and mark what changed between views. Position and area imply no measured amount.
Inputs needed
The same source condition in both views, with checked descriptions for each layer.
Tradeoffs
A toggle asks readers to remember the previous view. Offer a simultaneous still comparison.
One worked example
  • Question: Which parts of a photo does a detector rely on to call it a dog?
  • Made-up data: One photo; a second layer highlights the regions the detector weighted most: the ears and nose, not the grass.
  • What the reader can establish: With the photo fixed and only the layer changing, the call can be seen to rest on the head.
Used by, for example
  • Build from parts: Each added block lays its contribution over the same input, so the learner sees what that block changed.
  • Draw freehand: The learner's outline and the method's as two layers over one photo, with the gap shaded.
  • Select channels and reconstruct: The rebuild laid over the original, so what was lost shows as the uncovered part.
  • Cover it up: The covers on one layer and the model's response on another, over the same unchanged photo.

Source mechanics: Wonky · A visual history of every Air Jordan · The Naked Truth

02 · Many-to-many diagram

Linked category lanes

Select an item and follow its connections across stable categories.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Select an item and follow its connections across stable categories.
What the marks mean
Equal-width connectors show reviewed associations, including many-to-many links. Say what a connection means: an association, a sequence and a cause each need different treatment.
Inputs needed
An explicit relation table that permits several links and unresolved classifications.
Tradeoffs
Crossing lines grow quickly. Reveal a selected neighborhood; widths imply quantities only when justified.
One worked example
  • Question: Which senses does each food word describe?
  • Made-up data: Crisp links to texture and sound, sour to taste, smoky to smell and taste. A link means the word is used to describe that sense.
  • What the reader can establish: Two of the three words cross into two lanes, so a single-category label would hide them.
Used by, for example
  • Route and defer: Cases flowing into three stable lanes: kept, reviewed and discarded.
  • Ride along: One token's path across a lane per stage, with the other tokens' paths faint.

Source mechanics: How do animals sound across languages? · Wonky

03 · Comparison layout

Matched small multiples

Inspect repeated views beside one another.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Inspect repeated views beside one another.
What the marks mean
Consistent framing and marks; quantitative panels share a valid scale.
Inputs needed
Matched source conditions and a reason to compare the panels.
Tradeoffs
Too many panels dilute the comparison. Keep a recurring example and a small first view.
One worked example
  • Question: Does a smaller learning rate train more steadily?
  • Made-up data: Three loss curves on shared axes, 50 steps and loss from 0 to 2. Rate 0.1 swings between 0.6 and 1.4; rate 0.01 falls smoothly to 0.3; rate 0.001 is still at 1.2 at step 50.
  • What the reader can establish: Only the middle rate is both steady and low by step 50.
Used by, for example
  • Call the run: Three photos in matched frames; after the call, each frame shows the detector's boxes, so the miss is found by comparing like with like.
  • Mix to match: Camera-only, microphone-only and blended tracks in matched panels on one time axis.
  • Tune a process: Runs under three settings in matched panels on the same axes, so the effect of a setting is a comparison rather than a memory.
  • Select channels and reconstruct: The rebuild from each choice of channels in matched panels beside the original.
  • Scrub the clock: A filmstrip of frames at fixed steps in matched framing, with the handle selecting one.
  • Fork a run: The two branches in synchronized panels from the fork onward, with an optional difference view.
  • Listen: Each version's pitch line in matched panels on one time axis, under its play button.
  • Squint: The same image at a row of blur levels, matched in size.

Source mechanics: Women's Pockets are Inferior · How do animals sound across languages?

04 · Presentation pattern: disclosure

Expanding context

Open a short label into the circumstances needed to understand it, then fold it back.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Open a short label into the circumstances needed to understand it, then fold it back.
What the marks mean
Nested context and a persistent label. Panel size and phrase length are editorial layout.
Inputs needed
Verified examples, exact attribution and checked explanations.
Tradeoffs
A reveal can become a wall of text. Let a few precise clauses and source examples carry it.
One worked example
  • Question: Why does the word bank trouble a bag-of-words model?
  • Made-up data: The label bank opens into two sentences: "sat on the river bank" and "paid in at the bank".
  • What the reader can establish: Opened, the label shows one word carrying two meanings that a count treats as one.
Used by, for example
  • Find the fault: Each stage of the pipeline folds open to its input and output for the failing case.
  • Referee: Each output folds open to the rubric line behind the learner's score.

Source mechanics: The Structure of Stand-Up Comedy · A visual history of every Air Jordan

05 · Object atlas

Example contact sheet

Select one example in a contact sheet to inspect its condition, source and evidence.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Select one example in a contact sheet to inspect its condition, source and evidence.
What the marks mean
One tile per exact example; ordinary tile order and area imply no measured distance or intensity.
Inputs needed
Source-backed examples with stable IDs, distinct conditions, explicit omissions and a visible selection state.
Tradeoffs
Pictures can blur meaningful distinctions. Keep the original label and condition visible.
One worked example
  • Question: Which photos does a classifier get wrong, and under what condition?
  • Made-up data: Twelve tiles, each with its condition noted. Three are marked wrong, and all three are night shots.
  • What the reader can establish: The errors share a condition that is visible on their tiles.
Used by, for example
  • Seal the rule: The unseen cases open as a contact sheet, each tile marked pass or fail under the sealed rule.
  • Zoom: Zooming out turns single photos into a contact sheet; zooming in opens one tile with its boxes.
  • Teach a character: The candidate examples as a contact sheet, with the chosen five outlined during the test.
  • Spend a budget: Unlabelled images as a contact sheet; each bought label appears on its tile.

Source mechanics: Women's Pockets are Inferior · The Naked Truth

06 · Qualitative evidence lens

Usage in context

Compare an expression across genuine uses, then inspect each source.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Compare an expression across genuine uses, then inspect each source.
What the marks mean
Aligned wording and separately attributed contexts.
Inputs needed
Verified excerpts and source context, with qualified translation review where needed. Keep enough surrounding text to support the reading the page intends.
Tradeoffs
Exact language must earn its space. A few examples cannot establish frequency or a population claim.
One worked example
  • Question: Does sick mean ill?
  • Made-up data: Three aligned lines: "felt sick after lunch", "that trick was sick", "sick of waiting".
  • What the reader can establish: The word carries three senses here. Three lines cannot say which sense is most common.
Used by, for example
  • Type your own: The learner's sentence lined up with real sentences that share its key word, so the model's reading can be set against genuine uses.
  • Fool it: The edited sentence aligned with the original, the changed letters marked and the verdict beside each.
  • Be the model: The visible previous word with the real sentences that continue it, showing what the model has to go on.

Source mechanics: The Structure of Stand-Up Comedy · The Naked Truth

07 · Bipartite graph

Bipartite neighborhood

Trace between two named kinds of node, expanding one neighborhood at a time.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Trace between two named kinds of node, expanding one neighborhood at a time. Words and the examples that use them are one application.
What the marks mean
Two named node types and exact supplied links; equal-width links with counts on selection when available.
Inputs needed
A validated relation table, stable IDs and a guided first path.
Tradeoffs
A full network tangles quickly. Stable authored placement must not look like measured distance.
One worked example
  • Question: Which words appear in the same example sentences?
  • Made-up data: Words on one side and sentences on the other: cat in S1 and S2, dog in S2 and S3, car in S4.
  • What the reader can establish: Cat and dog connect through S2; car shares no sentence with either.
Used by, for example
  • Pair up: Captions on one side, photos on the other, and the learner's links drawn between them.
  • Resolve competing interpretations: Instructions on one side and the readings they allow on the other; the chosen example cuts the links to the unwanted reading.

Source mechanics: How Sonic DNA Connects Generations of Music

08 · Presentation pattern: linked highlighting

Object-first reveal

Start with a familiar example and reveal the concepts or labels linked to it.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Start with a familiar example and reveal the concepts or labels linked to it.
What the marks mean
Selection and documented association. Fading marks a filter state.
Inputs needed
Exact example labels, their documented links and a clear reset state.
Tradeoffs
The entry view hides the wider structure. Let readers reset and reverse the path.
One worked example
  • Question: What does a model link to this photo?
  • Made-up data: Selecting a lighthouse photo lights up its linked labels: tower 0.62, coast 0.21, rocket 0.05.
  • What the reader can establish: Starting from a familiar photo, the learner finds an unexpected label among the links.
Used by, for example
  • Teach a character: Selecting a test photo lights up the training examples most similar to it.
  • Listen: Selecting a mark plays its clip and highlights the matching stretch of the original.

09 · Pair evidence layout

Shared-example bridge

Bring two items together through the exact examples they share.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Bring two items together through the exact examples they share.
What the marks mean
Identity and membership in supplied lists; aligned positions are authored.
Inputs needed
Exact shared labels, original supporting values and known omission states.
Tradeoffs
An item missing from a supplied list is not necessarily absent. Avoid implying complete similarity. This is a focused view of a relation graph; link it to the full graph, such as a bipartite neighborhood.
One worked example
  • Question: Why does a model treat king and queen as similar?
  • Made-up data: Of five contexts shown for each word, three are shared: "the __ ruled", "crowned the __", "the __'s palace".
  • What the reader can establish: The similarity rests on three shared contexts, and two contexts for each word are not shared.
Used by, for example
  • Lay out a map: Selecting two words the learner placed far apart shows the examples they share.

Source mechanics: How Sonic DNA Connects Generations of Music · Women's Pockets are Inferior

10 · Paired bars / dots

Paired count strips

Compare paired bars or dots, then inspect the source record.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Compare paired bars or dots, then inspect the source record.
What the marks mean
Position or length on one scale, with original values and unknown markers for missing entries. Say whether the strips show counts, rates or measurements, and what makes two observations a pair, such as the same item under two conditions.
Inputs needed
Exact counts, units and source-collection limits.
Tradeoffs
Counts may lack a common denominator. Never invent percentages or turn omitted entries into zeros.
One worked example
  • Question: Did a fix reduce errors in every class?
  • Made-up data: Errors on the same 100 test images per class, before and after: cat 18 to 9, dog 12 to 11, bird 30 to 14. Each pair is one class on the same images.
  • What the reader can establish: Birds drop most and dogs barely move. The shared denominator makes the counts comparable.
Used by, for example
  • Place a bet: The learner's chips and the counts the text actually produced, as paired bars for each candidate word.
  • Rate, then see the crowd: For each image pair, the learner's rating, the crowd's and the model's as dots on one scale.
  • Rank: The learner's order and the model's as paired dots joined by lines, so the cards that moved stand out.
  • Route and defer: Each case's confidence as a dot on one scale, paired with its true outcome, with the review band drawn across.
  • Push until it breaks: For each difficulty step, paired dots for two methods, with failures marked where they occur.
  • Referee: The learner's score and the metric's as paired dots for each output.
  • Set a range: For each round, the range width and whether the outcome landed inside, as paired marks.

Source mechanics: Women's Pockets are Inferior

11 · Pairwise overview

Relationship matrix

Read one named pairwise relationship across a grid of cells.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Read one named pairwise relationship across a grid, opening a cell to its evidence.
What the marks mean
One named relationship and scale; each cell links to its supporting evidence.
Inputs needed
The complete intended matrix, explicit ordering and a consistent cell meaning.
Tradeoffs
A grid alone is an inspector. Explain one cell first; mirrored cells are not independent observations. Revealing one cell, then its row, then the grid is a presentation pattern that suits it.
One worked example
  • Question: Which classes does a classifier confuse?
  • Made-up data: True class in rows, predicted class in columns, 20 images per class. Correct: 17, 14 and 12. Fox predicted as dog: 6. Dog as fox: 4.
  • What the reader can establish: Most errors are between fox and dog. The cell meaning is stated once and holds for every cell.
Used by, for example
  • Sort into bins: Rows for the learner's piles and columns for the classifier's; each off-diagonal cell opens the photos they disagree on.
  • Pair up: A cost for every cup and saucer pairing in one grid, with the chosen pairs marked.
  • Repair without regressions: Rules as rows and earlier incidents as columns, each cell pass or fail, so a regression shows as one cell changing.
  • Choose the next experiment: Candidate rules as rows and possible inputs as columns, each cell a prediction; the useful inputs are the columns where the rows disagree.

Source mechanics: How Sonic DNA Connects Generations of Music · How the Words We Teach English Language Learners Changed

12 · Unit chart

Counted unit tiles

Gather or compare repeated marks, with the exact total available.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Gather or compare repeated marks, with the exact total available.
What the marks mean
A declared number of recorded units per mark.
Inputs needed
Exact counts, an explicit counting unit and consistent scaling.
Tradeoffs
Marks cannot silently change from mentions to people or independent samples. Large counts need a visible conversion. When tiles regroup, keep the counting unit and each tile's identity.
One worked example
  • Question: How many of 40 flagged emails were spam, and from whom?
  • Made-up data: 40 tiles: 26 spam, 14 not. Regrouped by sender: 10 from known senders (2 spam) and 30 from unknown senders (24 spam).
  • What the reader can establish: The total stays at 40 while the tiles regroup, and 8 of the 14 false flags come from known senders.
Used by, for example
  • Place a bet: Each chip is one tile, so a moved chip is a visible unit and the total of ten never hides.
  • Reshape a fixed total: The prize money as tiles moved between sectors, with the number of tiles fixed.
  • Cut the stream: Each piece as a tile, so the number of pieces against the budget stays visible.

Source mechanics: How the Words We Teach English Language Learners Changed

13 · UpSet

Intersection plot

Select a few sets, inspect an intersection and reveal its members.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Select a few sets, inspect an intersection and reveal its members.
What the marks mean
Membership dots plus counts, with individual set sizes retained. State which kind of intersection each bar counts: items in exactly these sets, the usual UpSet reading, or items in at least these sets.
Inputs needed
Exact set membership and explicit coverage or omission limits.
Tradeoffs
Readers must learn the notation. Use it for several sets; pairwise questions may need a simpler view.
One worked example
  • Question: Which problems occur together among failed images?
  • Made-up data: Sets: blurry (12 images), dark (9), cropped (6). Bars count images in exactly the marked sets: blurry only 5, dark only 2, cropped only 4, blurry and dark only 5, all three 2.
  • What the reader can establish: Blur and darkness often come together, and the images with all three problems are counted apart from those with two.
Used by, for example
  • Sort into bins: When a photo can sit in several piles, an intersection plot counts the photos in each combination.

14 · Projection / spatial navigation

Semantic neighborhood map

Explore nearby items while inspecting their original similarities and support.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Explore nearby items while inspecting their original similarities and support.
What the marks mean
Coordinates from named data and a named projection. A projection keeps some relations and distorts others: in t-SNE, cluster sizes and the gaps between clusters can mislead (How to Use t-SNE Effectively).
Inputs needed
A complete intended reference, verified labels and checks of projection distortion.
Tradeoffs
Nearby points can invite stronger claims than the data support. Keep original relationships available and state distortion.
One worked example
  • Question: Which words does a model place near apple?
  • Made-up data: A t-SNE map of 30 words puts apple between a fruit cluster (pear, plum) and a phone cluster (iPhone, charger). Original cosine similarities: apple and pear 0.71, apple and iPhone 0.64.
  • What the reader can establish: Apple belongs to both neighborhoods. The gap between the clusters is not a measured distance, so the original similarities settle it.
Used by, for example
  • Lay out a map: The learner's layout beside the model's projection of the same words.
  • Walk the space: A walker crossing a semantic map, each step revealing the original items near it.

Source mechanics: Use the reference and projection checks described above.

15 · Baseline comparison

Observed against reference

Set what happened against what would be expected, with the baseline open to inspection.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Compare an observed distribution with a reference one, then open the reference to see how it was built.
What the marks mean
Both distributions on one scale, the reference drawn as an outline and labelled with how it was computed.
Inputs needed
Observed values and a defined reference, such as the overall mix of all items, with its source.
Tradeoffs
A baseline carries assumptions. Show how it was built; a gap from an unexplained baseline shows nothing.
One worked example
  • Question: Are a detector's misses concentrated at night?
  • Made-up data: Misses by time of day, set against the share of all photos taken at each time. Night holds 20% of the photos and 45% of the misses.
  • What the reader can establish: Night misses run at more than twice their share. The reference shows what the misses would look like if they simply followed the photos.
Used by, for example
  • Rate, then see the crowd: The learner's rating placed on the distribution of everyone's ratings, with that reference's source one click away.
  • Reshape a fixed total: The reshaped distribution drawn over the original as a dashed outline, so what moved shows against where it started.

Source mechanics: The Naked Truth

16 · Uncertainty view

Outcomes with an interval band

Show the spread of outcomes and the range a claim covers.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Read where outcomes fall against a stated range, and count how often they land inside.
What the marks mean
Outcomes as dots on one scale and a shaded band for the range, with its definition and width labelled.
Inputs needed
Outcomes from data or a computation, and the band's definition, such as a 90% interval.
Tradeoffs
A band invites reading everything inside it as certain. Show the outcomes that fall outside, and count them.
One worked example
  • Question: Does a forecaster's 90% band hold?
  • Made-up data: Twenty days, each with a band of plus or minus 3 degrees around the forecast. Seventeen actual temperatures fall inside and three outside, all three on the warm side.
  • What the reader can establish: Coverage is 17 of 20, a little under the stated 90%, and the misses lean one way.
Used by, for example
  • Set a range: Each committed range drawn as a band over the outcomes that followed, with the misses counted.
  • Tune a process: Many runs under one setting drawn as a band around their average path, so the spread is part of the result.

17 · Execution trace

Event lanes with a state table

Line up events from several processes in time, beside the state they change.

Action, evidence and tradeoffs
Reader action
Step through events across lanes and watch a shared state table update.
What the marks mean
One lane per process, each event a mark in time order, and a table of the shared state after each step.
Inputs needed
An exact event log, or a simulation with a stated order of events, and the state each event reads and writes.
Tradeoffs
Lanes crowd quickly. Show two or three processes and the few state values that matter.
One worked example
  • Question: How can two charges leave a balance as if only one happened?
  • Made-up data: Processes A and B each charge 30 to a balance of 100. A reads 100, B reads 100, A writes 70, B writes 70.
  • What the reader can establish: The state table shows 70 where 40 was due, and the step where B writes over A's change is the one to inspect.
Used by, for example
  • Interleave events: One lane per process and the shared state beside them, so the step that breaks the requirement is in view.
  • Repair without regressions: The failing incident's events in lanes beside the account's state, so the step the new rule changes is visible.
  • Find the fault: Each stage as a lane with the values it passes on, so the first wrong value marks the faulty stage.

Presentation patterns

Two entries above, and part of a third, are ways of presenting rather than visual forms. Each works with most methods and most actions.

  • Expanding context is disclosure: a short label opens into its circumstances and folds back.
  • Object-first reveal is linked highlighting: selecting one item lights up what is linked to it, on any form.
  • Progressive reveal, as in the relationship matrix, shows one cell, then its row, then the whole grid. The same order suits graphs, maps and contact sheets.

Give the visual a job in the unit.

The same visual method can serve different activities. Choose its role in the learning sequence before designing its controls.

Unit opener

Expose the question before its vocabulary. Give learners a simple decision whose consequence makes a new idea useful.

Mid-lecture explainer

Make a mechanism just taught visible. Compare two states, then check understanding on a case the readout does not show.

Unit summary

Hold one example across the unit's methods, with each step addressing a visible limitation. Place it before the problem set and keep it unassessed.

Lab evidence view

Let learners inspect a controlled comparison and the evidence behind a claim. Preserve units, uncertainty, missingness and access to original values.
